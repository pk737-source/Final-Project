White Flight & Urban Decline

After World War II, many white families left Newark for the suburbs while jobs and investment followed them out. The result was a shrinking tax base, aging housing, and growing inequality in a city that had once been among the most prosperous in the region.

Policies That Pushed People Out

Federal housing programs and bank lending practices in the mid-1900s made it far easier for white families to buy homes in the suburbs, while Black families were often shut out of both suburban neighborhoods and home loans. At the same time, new highways and urban renewal projects cleared parts of the city and displaced thousands of residents.

  • Redlining and discriminatory lending limited who could buy where
  • Highway construction and urban renewal demolished neighborhoods
  • Factories closed or moved away, taking thousands of jobs with them
  • The loss of tax revenue strained schools, housing, and city services

The Numbers

Newark's white population fell by more than half between 1950 and 1970, and the city's total population dropped from about 440,000 to about 380,000. Housing conditions in the Central Ward worsened, public housing grew crowded, and unemployment rose. Many residents felt shut out of the decisions that were shaping their neighborhoods.

Building Toward 1967

These pressures built for years before they boiled over. The events of July 1967 are covered on their own page, which is listed under this one in the navigation menu.
